# Pilot Script — Why Can a City Stay Hot After Sunset?

**Status:** Research-backed first draft; not yet approved for recording  
**Target duration:** 75–90 seconds  
**Narration:** British English male storyteller  
**Format:** 16:9, 1920×1080, 24 fps  
**Visual language:** Stick-figure presenters, hand-drawn city illustrations, restrained photo inserts

## Narration and picture plan

### 00:00–00:10 — The uncomfortable evening
**MAYA:** “The sun has gone down. So why does this street still feel like an oven?”  
**Picture:** Maya fans herself beside a road. Heat shimmer rises from the asphalt; the sky darkens.

### 00:10–00:23 — The city stores the day
**LEO:** “Because a city is covered in materials that soak up sunlight. Roads, roofs and walls absorb energy through the day, then release some of that stored heat after sunset.”  
**Picture:** Sunlight arrows hit a dark road and rooftops. The surfaces glow, then emit slow orange heat waves at night.

### 00:23–00:37 — Less natural cooling
**MAYA:** “And where have the trees gone?”  
**LEO:** “Vegetation cools its surroundings with shade and by moving water from soil and leaves into the air. Replace much of that greenery with hard, dry surfaces, and you lose some of that cooling.”  
**Picture:** Split-screen: tree canopy shades a pavement; on the other side, bare concrete bakes. Simple water-vapour arrows rise from leaves.

### 00:37–00:50 — A city is not one temperature
**MAYA:** “So every street is equally hot?”  
**LEO:** “Not at all. A shaded park, a tree-lined street and a wide paved car park can feel very different. Building layout and airflow matter too.”  
**Picture:** Camera travels from a shaded park to a dense street canyon and then an exposed car park. A simple thermometer changes, with no fabricated numbers.

### 00:50–01:05 — The extra heat
**LEO:** “Vehicles, buildings and air-conditioning systems can add waste heat as well. The result is an urban heat island: built-up places can be warmer than nearby less-developed areas.”  
**Picture:** Small icons for a car, building and outdoor AC unit release heat; a simple city silhouette sits above a cooler green landscape.

### 01:05–01:20 — The useful question
**MAYA:** “Can we make cities cooler?”  
**LEO:** “We can help. More shade and vegetation, greener roofs, and more reflective roofs and pavements can all reduce heat in the right conditions. The best choice depends on the place.”  
**Picture:** Trees appear along the street; a roof turns light-coloured; a green roof grows in. Heat waves soften, not vanish.

### 01:20–01:27 — Close
**MAYA:** “So the heat isn't just in the weather. It's also in what we've built.”  
**Picture:** Maya and Leo face the cooler street. End card: “A little science. A clearer picture.”

## Editorial and fact-check notes

- Do not imply that every city is always hotter than every rural location; the effect varies with climate, weather, geography, season and urban form.
- Do not use the commonly quoted US temperature ranges as universal figures or apply them to an Indian city without local evidence.
- Keep surface temperature and air temperature distinct in graphics and narration.
- Do not imply trees solve every heat problem or that reflective surfaces work identically in every setting.
- The dialogue is a draft for timing and story structure. Final narration requires a human editorial pass and source review.
